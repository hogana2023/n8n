Ask someone where their food waste comes from and they'll usually say leftovers. Plate scrapings, the end of a curry nobody wanted on day three.

The numbers say otherwise. Leftovers are a small slice. The bulk of what gets binned was never cooked at all.

## The four categories

Across households tracking their pantry for ninety days, waste clusters into four groups:

1. **Bought for one recipe, used once.** The half-bunch of coriander, the rest of the tin of chipotle, the buttermilk. Bought deliberately, used deliberately, then stranded.
2. **Bought on optimism.** Vegetables purchased by the person you intend to be. Usually leafy, usually green, usually found three weeks later.
3. **Duplicates.** Two jars of the same thing because you couldn't remember whether you had one. Cheap individually, relentless in aggregate.
4. **Genuine leftovers.** Real, but the smallest of the four.

The first three have a common feature: they're all failures of memory rather than failures of appetite. Nobody decided to waste that coriander. They just forgot it existed.

## Why memory is the bottleneck

A typical kitchen holds somewhere between forty and eighty distinct edible things at once, spread across a fridge, a freezer, and two or three cupboards at different heights. Some are visible, most aren't.

You cannot hold that inventory in your head, and the kitchen isn't designed to help. Things at the back don't exist. Things behind other things don't exist. The freezer is a black box with a light in it.

So you cook from the twelve things you can see, buy more of those twelve things, and the other sixty quietly age.

## The intervention that works

Making the invisible things visible fixes most of it. Not an elaborate system, just a list that knows what you have and when it's likely to turn.

Two changes follow almost immediately:

- Duplicates stop, because you check before you buy.
- Optimism purchases get used, because they show up in suggestions while they're still good.

That handles categories one through three, which is most of the problem. Leftovers you'll still have to eat yourself.

## A reasonable target

Households that stick with it land around a third less waste after three months. Not zero. Zero would require a level of discipline that nobody sustains, and chasing it is how these systems get abandoned.

A third is achievable, it holds, and at current prices it's roughly £50 a month back in your pocket.
